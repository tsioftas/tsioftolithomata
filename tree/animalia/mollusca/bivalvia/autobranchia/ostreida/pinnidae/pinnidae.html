    Pinnidae (pen shells, fan mussels) are a family of large marine bivalves with a distinctively elongated, wedge- or fan-shaped shell that tapers toward the pointed base. They live vertically embedded in sediment, with the apex anchored in sand or mud and tethered by byssal threads to buried hard objects, filtering food through enlarged gills. Some species are among the largest of all bivalves, exceeding a metre in length. The family has existed since the Carboniferous, retaining its general form almost unchanged. Their shells preserve readily as fossils, often showing the distinctive longitudinal ribbing.

    Etymology

    “Pinnidae” from the type genus Pinna, of which it is the family. Pinna is an ancient Greek word, πίννα, attested in Aristotle's History of Animals for the pen shell (Pinna nobilis) which grows vertically rooted in the seafloor; probably of pre-Greek origin. The genus Pinna was erected by Linnaeus in 1758, the family Pinnidae by the English zoologist William Elford Leach in 1819.
