    Gryphaea is an extinct genus of oysters, common in Jurassic and Early Cretaceous marine deposits. Known for their thick, strongly curved shells, Gryphaea had a large, coiled left valve and a flatter right valve that acted as a lid. This distinct shape helped anchor them in soft, muddy seafloors.

    They were sedentary suspension feeders, similar to modern oysters, and often formed dense populations known as “Gryphaea beds.” Compared to other oysters, they are notable for their extreme shell asymmetry and curvature. Their unusual appearance has earned them the nickname "Devil’s Toenails" and made them useful as index fossils.

    Etymology

    “Gryphaea” from Greek γρύψ (grýps, gen. γρυπός, ‘griffin’ — the mythic eagle-headed beast known for its hooked beak), referring to the strongly curved, hook-shaped lower valve resembling a griffin’s claw or talon. The genus was erected by Lamarck in 1801. In British folklore the fossils are popularly called ‘devil’s toenails’ for the same resemblance.
