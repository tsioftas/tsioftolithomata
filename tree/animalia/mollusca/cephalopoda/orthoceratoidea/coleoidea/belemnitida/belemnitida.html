    Belemnitida were extinct coleoid cephalopods that thrived in the Jurassic and Early Cretaceous. Unlike ammonoids with their external coiled shells, belemnites possessed an internal shell ending in a solid calcitic rostrum, usually cigar-shaped and often the only part preserved as a fossil.

    They resembled squids in overall form but differed by bearing ten arms with hooks instead of suckers and by their heavy rostrum rather than the flexible gladius of modern squids. Fast-swimming and predatory, they hunted fish and invertebrates, while their rostra, preserved in vast numbers, serve as important index fossils for Mesozoic strata.

    Belemnites declined in the Late Cretaceous and disappeared in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction, leaving squids, cuttlefish, and octopuses as their surviving coleoid relatives.

    Etymology

    “Belemnitida” from Greek βέλεμνον (bélemnon, ‘dart, javelin, projectile’) — after the characteristic bullet- or dart-shaped calcitic ‘rostrum’ (the internal hard part) that is also the most commonly preserved element. Introduced as a taxonomic name by d'Orbigny in 1840. In many folk traditions the rostra were called ‘thunderbolts’ or ‘devil's fingers’, believed to have fallen from the sky during storms.
