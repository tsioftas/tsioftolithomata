    Nummulites is an extinct genus of larger benthic foraminifera that flourished from the late Paleocene to the early Oligocene. Each individual occupied a flat or lens-shaped, planispirally coiled calcareous test containing many chambers and an internal canal system. The life cycle produced two markedly different generations: small initial chambers generally developed into large tests, whereas large initial chambers produced smaller adults. Most tests are millimetres to a few centimetres across, but exceptional species exceeded ten centimetres, making them among the largest shelled single-celled organisms known. Nummulites hosted photosynthetic algae and lived on warm, sunlit carbonate shelves. During the Eocene their tests accumulated into extensive nummulitic limestones across the Tethyan region; such limestone occurs in the blocks of the Giza pyramids and inspired ancient stories that the fossils were petrified lentils.

    Etymology

    “Nummulites” derives from Latin nummulus, the diminutive of nummus (“coin”), with the taxonomic suffix -ites, referring to the test's coin-like form. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck established the genus in 1801.
