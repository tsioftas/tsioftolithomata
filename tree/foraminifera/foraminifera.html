    Phylum Foraminifera

    Foraminifera, or forams, are predominantly marine single-celled eukaryotes within Rhizaria. Their defining feature is a branching and reconnecting network of granular pseudopodia, used for movement, feeding and construction of the shell, or test. Test composition is highly diverse: some forms secrete calcium carbonate, others cement selected sediment grains together, and early-branching lineages may have organic or no tests. Most species live on or within the seabed, while several lineages independently entered the plankton. Probable simple foraminifera occur in Cambrian rocks, while the oldest unequivocal multichambered tests are Middle Ordovician; mineralised tests become abundant in many younger marine deposits. Rapid evolutionary change, broad distributions and sensitivity to environmental conditions make forams exceptionally useful for dating strata and reconstructing ancient water depth, temperature, chemistry and climate.

    Etymology

    “Foraminifera” combines Latin foramen (genitive foraminis, “opening”) with ferre (“to bear”), hence “opening-bearers”. The name refers primarily to the openings between successive chambers of a multichambered test, not to wall pores, because many foraminiferal tests are imperforate. Alcide d'Orbigny introduced the group in 1826.
