# About the excavation

This excavation is organised by the [Tertiary Research Group (TRG)](https://www.trg.org/), a group of amateur and professional palaeontologists who also work with the [Natural History Museum in London](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/). They're looking for fossil remains of [birds](../tree/animalia/chordata/osteichthyes/sarcopterygii/sauropsida/dinosauria/theropoda/aves/aves), reptiles and [mammals](../tree/animalia/chordata/osteichthyes/sarcopterygii/synapsida/mammalia/mammalia). Participants can keep the other finds ([shark teeth](../tree/animalia/chordata/chondrichthyes/elasmobranchii/elasmobranchii), fish bones and shells) for their own collections.

There's more about the area's geology and fossils on the [Abbey Wood locality page](../localities/abbey-wood).

The nearest station is Abbey Wood. From there, it's about a ten-minute walk to the park where the ruins of Lesnes Abbey stand.

This was once a monastery. According to the [abbey's history](https://www.lesnesabbeywoods.org/explore/history/), Cardinal Wolsey closed it in 1525, before the wider dissolution of the monasteries under Henry VIII. Judging by the ruins, it must have been quite a building in its day.

# The excavation

The place was full of buckets, tubs, sieves and bags. People were working down in the mud, some at their sieves, others bent over the material, looking for fossils.

Behind the fence was a small hired digger, brought in to reach the fossil-bearing layers below. The excavated material was heaped into a little mound. Everyone went over with their buckets, shovelled them full and carried them back for sieving.

## Washing and sieving

The idea was to wash the sand away from the fossils and bag up the finer material left behind (fragments, little stones, everything) to take home and sort through. I picked out the larger pieces, such as big teeth, shells and bones, and put them in the containers I'd brought along.

# The finds

The photos show two containers of finds: one with teeth and other vertebrate remains, the other with shells. The shark teeth stand out in the first, with their shiny points and forked roots. There are also a few vertebrae, a small piece of [turtle shell](../tree/animalia/chordata/osteichthyes/sarcopterygii/sauropsida/testudines/testudines) and a little bone from a fish's tail (a hypural bone).

I put the shells in a separate container. There are both [gastropods](../tree/animalia/mollusca/gastropoda/gastropoda) and [bivalves](../tree/animalia/mollusca/bivalvia/bivalvia), mostly complete; there were plenty of broken bits that weren't worth picking up. Even the common finds have something to show you if you give them a little attention.
